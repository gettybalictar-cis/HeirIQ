# Philippine Estate Planning — Advisory Reference
*Compiled for advisory use. Not legal advice — verify current figures with BIR/counsel before client action. Current as of August 2026.*

---

## 1. The Four Pillars of PH Estate Planning

| Pillar | Governing Law | What It Controls |
|---|---|---|
| Succession / who gets what | Civil Code (Arts. 774–1105) | Legitime, compulsory heirs, wills, intestacy |
| Marital property | Family Code (EO 209, 1988) | What's "in" the estate vs. spouse's own |
| Transfer tax while alive | NIRC as amended by TRAIN (RA 10963) | Donor's tax |
| Transfer tax at death | NIRC as amended by TRAIN (RA 10963) | Estate tax |

The core advisory tension: **civil law tells you what heirs are *entitled* to (legitime); tax law tells you what it *costs* to move property to them, alive or dead.** Good planning sequences the two — using the free portion and the annual donor's tax exemption to shift value early, without breaching legitime.

---

## 2. Marital Property Regime — Threshold: Marriage Date & Prenup

This determines what's actually *in* the decedent's estate before you even get to succession.

- **Default regime (no prenup, married on/after Aug 3, 1988):** Absolute Community of Property (ACP) — nearly everything owned before and during marriage is pooled. Exceptions: property acquired by gratuitous title (inheritance, donation) during marriage stays exclusive, unless the donor/testator says otherwise.
- **Marriages before Aug 3, 1988 (no prenup):** Conjugal Partnership of Gains (CPG) — pre-marriage property stays separate; only *gains* during marriage are pooled.
- **Prenup executed before marriage:** Can elect CPG, Complete Separation of Property, or a hybrid — but **must be executed before the wedding**; changing regimes after marriage requires a *court petition* (Art. 76 FC), which is slow and public.
- **Muslim marriages (PD 1083):** separation of property can apply by default.

**Advisory relevance:** Before computing legitime or estate tax, you must first split the estate into (a) the decedent's exclusive property and (b) their half of the community/conjugal property. Only (a) + (b) forms the "hereditary estate." Misclassifying a conjugal asset as 100% the decedent's is one of the most common and costly estate-settlement errors — it inflates the taxable estate and can shortchange the surviving spouse's own share (which is *not* inherited, it's simply returned to them as owner).

**Threshold to flag:** Once a couple did not sign a prenup before marrying, the regime is fixed — the only fix later is a court-approved separation of property, which is expensive and adversarial. If a client wants asset segregation (e.g., pre-marital business, family inheritance protection) and is *not yet married*, this is a narrow, use-it-or-lose-it window.

## 3. Legitime — What Cannot Be Willed Away

Legitime is the reserved portion the law guarantees to **compulsory heirs**, regardless of what a will says. A will (or lifetime donation) that impairs legitime can be reduced/annulled by an aggrieved heir (*accion de reduccion*, Art. 907, 4-year+ prescriptive issues aside).

**Compulsory heirs (in order/concurrence):**
1. Legitimate children/descendants
2. Legitimate parents/ascendants (only if no children)
3. Surviving spouse (always concurs with 1 or 2)
4. Illegitimate children (always get their share; smaller than legitimate)

**Key shares (Civil Code Arts. 888–903):**
- Legitimate children collectively: **1/2 of the hereditary estate** (the "legitime"), divided equally among them. The other 1/2 is the **free portion** the testator can dispose of by will however they wish.
- Surviving spouse (with legitimate children): share equal to *one legitimate child's* share, taken from the free portion.
- Illegitimate child: **1/2 the share of a legitimate child** (must not exceed the free portion; spouse's legitime is satisfied first).
- No children, spouse + illegitimate children only: spouse gets 1/3, illegitimate children share 1/3, remaining 1/3 free.
- No compulsory heirs at all: testator can dispose of the *entire* estate by will.

**Advisory relevance / where disputes come from:**
- Illegitimate children are compulsory heirs too — omitting them from planning (common when families want to avoid "complications") is a legal error, not just a social one, and is a top source of post-death litigation.
- The **free portion (up to 1/2 the estate)** is the actual lever an adviser has for customizing outcomes — favoring a caregiver child, funding a foundation, rewarding a business successor, etc. Planning conversations should center on *how to use the free portion*, since the legitime is non-negotiable.
- Disinheritance is possible only on narrow statutory grounds (Art. 919) and must be stated in a valid will — "informal" disinheritance (just leaving someone out) does not work; the omitted compulsory heir can still claim legitime.

## 4. Collation — Why Lifetime Gifts Still Count at Death

Collation (Civil Code Arts. 1061–1077) is the mechanism that stops lifetime donations from being used to dodge legitime. It is the direct answer to "what happens to an asset the owner already gave away before they died."

**The rule:** Any donation the decedent made *during their lifetime to a compulsory heir* is treated as an **advance on that heir's inheritance**, not a separate bonus. When legitime is computed after death, the value of that gift is added back — fictitiously, on paper — to the net estate to determine the true base for everyone's legitime (Art. 908). The heir who already received it then gets a correspondingly smaller share of what's left.

**Mechanics:**
- **Art. 908:** Net estate at death + value of all collatable lifetime donations = the base used to compute legitime for all compulsory heirs.
- **Art. 909:** Donations to a *child* are charged against that child's own legitime. Donations to a *stranger* (non-compulsory-heir, e.g., a friend or a foundation) are charged against the *free portion* instead.
- **Art. 1061:** Collation is a bookkeeping fiction, not a physical return — the donee does not hand the asset back. Their eventual inheritance share is simply reduced by what they already received.
- **Valuation is frozen at the time of donation** (Art. 1071; confirmed in *Vizconde v. CA*, G.R. No. 118449, 1998) — not current market value. If the gifted asset later appreciates, that upside belongs entirely to the donee and is not clawed back into the estate math. This is what makes early, transparent gifting a genuinely effective planning tool rather than a wash.

**What is NOT subject to (or exempt from) collation:**
- Donations to **strangers** are not collated the same way as heir-to-heir advances, but they still reduce the free portion, and can be *reduced* by aggrieved heirs if they impair legitime.
- The decedent can **expressly exclude** a donation from collation (Art. 1062) — but this only works if the gift still fits within the free portion. If it exceeds the free portion regardless of the exclusion clause, the excess is still an "inofficious donation" subject to reduction (Art. 771, 907).
- Certain customary/statutory carve-outs exist: wedding gifts within limits, support/education expenses, and donations to grandchildren by representation (Arts. 1062, 1064) receive special treatment — verify specifics case by case.
- Genuine arm's-length **sales** are not donations and are not collated — but a sale priced well below fair market value can be recharacterized as a disguised partial donation if challenged, and pulled back into collation on the discounted portion.

**Advisory implications:**
1. **"Gift it early to get it out of the estate" does not defeat legitime.** It only pre-pays it. Any adviser suggesting lifetime donation as a way to disinherit-by-stealth is setting the client up for a later legitime-impairment claim from the other heirs — this needs to be said plainly to clients who ask for this.
2. **Documentation is the deliverable.** Every lifetime donation to a child should be captured with a clear date and valuation at the time of transfer (deed of donation, BIR Form 1800 filing, appraisal if needed). This is what makes collation at death a clean arithmetic exercise instead of a valuation fight among siblings.
3. **This is what makes the ₱250,000/year donor's-tax-exempt gifting strategy (Section 6) safe for family harmony**, not just tax-efficient — if gifts to each child are made proportionally and transparently over time, collation nets out evenly at death and there is little left to dispute.
4. **To genuinely favor one heir beyond their legitime**, the only clean route is a donation with an express non-collation clause *and* confirmation, at the time of the gift, that it stays within the current free portion. Even then, this should be revisited periodically — the free portion is a moving target as the estate's value and heir composition change.
5. **Frozen valuation rewards early action on appreciating assets** — this reinforces the point in Section 6: gifting appreciating real property early both minimizes tax exposure and locks in a lower value for collation purposes, benefiting the donee-heir without shortchanging co-heirs (their legitime is still measured fairly, just at the earlier value).

## 5. Estate Tax (Death) — TRAIN Law, RA 10963

| Item | Figure |
|---|---|
| Rate | Flat **6%** of net taxable estate (was 5–20% graduated pre-2018) |
| Standard deduction | **₱5,000,000** automatic, no documentation |
| Family home deduction | Up to **₱10,000,000** (the actual FMV or ₱10M, whichever is lower) |
| Funeral expenses | Up to ₱200,000 (deductible only for pre-2018 deaths under old rules; TRAIN simplified this — verify current BIR RR before relying on it for post-2018 deaths) |
| De minimis exemption | Estates at or below ₱200,000 net: no tax |
| Filing deadline | **1 year from date of death**, BIR Form 1801; extendable up to 30 days (or longer in meritorious cases, subject to BIR approval — some sources cite up to 5 years for extensions in specific circumstances) |
| Late filing penalty | 25% surcharge (50% if fraud) + 12%/year interest (20%/year for the pre-2018 portion under old NIRC rules, per TRAIN transition) |
| CPA certification | Required when **gross estate exceeds ₱5,000,000** |
| Bank withdrawal | No longer capped at ₱20,000 (old rule) — any amount allowed, subject to **6% final withholding tax** at source before eCAR issuance |

**Threshold math that matters for advisory positioning:**
- A married couple's family home + ₱5M standard deduction can shelter up to **₱15M** of net estate value from tax entirely (₱5M standard + ₱10M family home cap) — before even factoring the surviving spouse's own 50% share of community property, which was never part of the decedent's estate to begin with. Getting a client to *understand this stacking* often reveals their "estate tax problem" is smaller than feared, which reduces panic-driven bad decisions (e.g., risky property transfers to dodge a tax that mostly wouldn't apply anyway).
- **₱5,000,000 gross estate** is the line that triggers mandatory CPA certification — a real cost/complexity threshold. Estates hovering just above this line are worth structuring (via lifetime donations, discussed below) to stay under it if that avoids professional fees disproportionate to the estate's complexity.
- **Estate Tax Amnesty (RA 11213, extended by RA 11569, RA 11956) expired June 14, 2025** and has **not** been renewed as of mid-2026, though House Bill 6614 / Senate Bill 1866 (extending to Dec 31, 2028, covering deaths through Dec 31, 2024) is pending in Congress. **This is time-sensitive and must be verified at the point of engagement** — if it passes, any client with an unsettled pre-2022/pre-2024 estate should move immediately to capture the reduced amnesty rate before another possible lapse. Advisory action: check status before every new estate-settlement engagement; don't assume it's open or closed.

## 6. Donor's Tax (Lifetime Transfers) — TRAIN Law

| Item | Figure |
|---|---|
| Rate | Flat **6%** on net gifts, uniform regardless of relationship to donee (relatives and "strangers" now taxed the same) |
| Annual exemption | **₱250,000 per donor per calendar year** — cumulative across *all* donees, not per-recipient |
| Filing | Donor files **BIR Form 1800 within 30 days** of each donation |
| Additional exemptions | Donations to accredited charitable/religious/educational/social welfare orgs (with ≤30% used for admin costs); donations to government; wedding gifts from parents to children up to ₱10,000 (per Civil Code, not inflation-adjusted, so mostly symbolic today) |
| "Contemplation of death" trap | Donations made and death occurring **within roughly the same period around death** can be pulled back into the *estate* tax base (NIRC Sec. 85(B)) rather than taxed as a lifetime gift — defeats the point of donating early |

**The core loophole/strategy — and its limits:**

Because estate tax and donor's tax are both flat 6%, the *rate* is not where the savings come from. The savings come from three places:
1. **The ₱250,000/year exemption is renewable every calendar year.** A parent can gift ₱250,000 worth of value tax-free to each child (or spread across children) *every single year*, indefinitely, with zero donor's tax. Over a decade, this alone can move a meaningful amount of an estate's value out of the future taxable pool.
2. **Locking in today's value.** Real property tends to appreciate, and BIR zonal values get revised upward over time. Donating now taxes today's (lower) value; waiting until death taxes tomorrow's (higher) value against the same 6% rate. This is the single biggest lever for appreciating assets (land, especially).
3. **Avoiding estate settlement friction entirely** for the donated asset — no publication, no CPA cert threshold contribution, no risk of an heir dispute over that particular asset since it's already transferred and accepted.

**Where this strategy breaks, and why the adviser must flag it:**
- **Legitime is not avoided by donating** — lifetime donations to compulsory heirs are treated as **advances on legitime** (Art. 1061, "collation") and get added back into the notional estate at settlement to check whether legitime was respected. Donating heavily to one child "off the books" to favor them doesn't work; it gets clawed back into the accounting, and can trigger a legitime-impairment claim from other heirs.
- Donations that impair the legitime of compulsory heirs are **reducible** (other heirs can sue to claw back the excess) — this is a major harmony risk if not planned transparently.
- The **one-year-before-death lookback** can convert a donation back into an estate-tax item, so donation as a deathbed tax move is unreliable — this only works as a *long-horizon* strategy, not a crisis move.
- Donating real property still triggers **local transfer tax** and, depending on structure, potentially capital gains tax exposure if disguised as a sale — advisers should not blur "donation" and "sale at a discount" (a bargain sale below FMV can itself be treated as a partial donation and taxed on the discount, per NIRC Sec. 100).

**Advisory strategy takeaway:** For clients years away from death with appreciating real property and multiple children, a **programmatic annual gifting plan** (₱250,000/year/donor, documented, collated transparently against legitime) is usually the single highest-value, lowest-cost estate planning move available — cheaper than eventual estate tax, avoids extrajudicial settlement friction for those assets, and if done openly and proportionately, preserves family harmony better than a surprise will.

## 7. Estate Settlement Mechanics — Where Time and Money Actually Get Spent

This is where "optimize costs, avoid inconvenient procedures" lives in practice — the tax computation is rarely the expensive part; the *settlement process* is.

**Extrajudicial Settlement (EJS) — the cheap, fast path (Rule 74, Rules of Court):**
Available only if: (a) decedent left **no will**, (b) **no outstanding debts** (or debts are settled/creditors consent), and (c) **all heirs are of legal age** (or minors are properly represented).
- Requires notarized public instrument (Deed of EJS, or Affidavit of Self-Adjudication if a single heir) signed by **all heirs**
- Must be **published once a week for 3 consecutive weeks** in a newspaper of general circulation
- A bond may be required if there's personal property, sized to its value
- Missing/omitted heirs (common with children from prior relationships) can void the settlement and expose participating heirs to liability — this is the most common defect
- Two-year window: settlements can be challenged by omitted heirs or creditors for **2 years** after publication/registration
- Typical timeline: 2–6 months if uncontested and documents are in order

**Judicial Settlement — the expensive, slow fallback:**
Required whenever there's a will (must go through **probate**, even an uncontested one) or unresolved disputes among heirs, or unpaid debts without creditor consent. Court-supervised, can take years, involves an appointed executor/administrator, and is where legal fees accumulate.

**Advisory positioning:** The single most effective process-cost lever an adviser controls is **keeping the family eligible for extrajudicial settlement** — i.e., encouraging a complete, accurate heir list and clean debt picture *before* death, and steering clients toward intestate-compatible planning (via lifetime donations and clear beneficiary designations) rather than a contested will, wherever the client's wishes can be achieved without one. A will is sometimes necessary (e.g., to use the free portion deliberately), but every will forces probate — so the adviser should weigh "customization via will" against "the guaranteed judicial settlement cost and delay that comes with having one" and consider whether lifetime donations + insurance beneficiary designations can achieve the same distributional goals without a will at all.

## 8. Practical Levers Advisers Should Actively Use

1. **Annual ₱250,000 donor's tax exemption, per donor, renewed yearly** — the highest-leverage recurring tool, especially for appreciating real estate. Multiple donors (both spouses) doubles the annual sheltered amount per recipient.
2. **Family home deduction (up to ₱10M)** — confirm the property is properly documented as *the* family home (actual residence, not just any owned house) to secure this.
3. **Life insurance with named beneficiaries** — proceeds generally pass outside the probate/EJS estate directly to the named beneficiary (subject to the policy's terms and whether the estate itself is named beneficiary), giving liquidity to pay estate taxes without forcing a fire-sale of illiquid assets, and sidestepping settlement delay for that value.
4. **Keeping the heir list complete and undisputed** — the cheapest way to guarantee eligibility for extrajudicial (not judicial) settlement.
5. **Sequencing donations early and consistently, not as a deathbed maneuver** — the 1-year lookback and collation rules punish last-minute moves.
6. **Watching the CPA-certification (₱5M gross estate) and standard-plus-family-home (₱15M combined) thresholds** when deciding how aggressively to donate down an estate before death — donating an estate from, say, ₱18M to ₱4.5M gross can eliminate both the CPA certification requirement and most of the tax.
7. **Monitor legislative status of the Estate Tax Amnesty** at the start of every engagement — its presence or absence changes the calculus entirely for any client with an already-deceased, unsettled predecessor's estate.
